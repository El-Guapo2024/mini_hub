# Multiplying by Any Fraction of 100, 1000, etc.

You can take what we learned from the 25's and 75's trick (converting them to fractions of 100) with a variety of potential fractions. $\frac{1}{8}\text{'s}$ are chosen often because:

$$
125 = \frac{1}{8} \cdot 1000 \qquad 37.5 = \frac{3}{8} \cdot 100 \qquad 6.25 = \frac{5}{8} \cdot 10
$$

In addition, you see $\frac{1}{6}\text{'s}$, $\frac{1}{3}\text{'s}$, $\frac{1}{9}\text{'s}$, and sometimes even $\frac{1}{12}\text{'s}$ for approximation problems (because they do not go evenly into 100, 1000, etc., they have to be approximated usually).

$$
\begin{aligned}
223 \approx \frac{2}{9} \cdot 1000 \\
8333.3 \approx \frac{5}{6} \cdot 10000 \approx \frac{1}{12} \cdot 100000 \\
327 \approx \frac{1}{3} \cdot 1000
\end{aligned}
$$

For approximations you will rarely ever see them equate to almost exactly to the correct fraction. For example you might use $\frac{2}{3} \cdot 1000$ for any value from $654 - 678$. Usually you can tell for the approximation problems what fraction the test writer is really going for.

## Executing the Actual Computation

Recognizing the fraction is only step one. On the test, you almost always still have to multiply or divide that fraction against some other number, or apply it as a percent of a value. The whole point of the recognition step is that it turns an ugly decimal multiplication into fast mental arithmetic: multiply by the numerator, divide by the denominator (in whichever order is easier), and keep track of the power of 10.

**Multiplying an eighths-number by another number.** $125 \times 320$: recognize $125 = \frac{1}{8} \cdot 1000$, so this is $\frac{1}{8} \cdot 1000 \cdot 320$. Divide 320 by 8 first (easy: $320 / 8 = 40\text{)}$, then multiply by 1000:

$$
125 \times 320 = \frac{320}{8} \cdot 1000 = 40 \cdot 1000 = 40000
$$

**Dividing by an eighths-number.** $138 \div 125$: since $125 = \frac{1}{8} \cdot 1000$, dividing by $125$ is the same as multiplying by $\frac{8}{1000}$. So:

$$
138 \div 125 = 138 \cdot \frac{8}{1000} = \frac{138 \cdot 8}{1000} = \frac{1104}{1000} = 1.104
$$

Here it was easier to multiply $138 \times 8$ first (getting $1104\text{)}$ and only then place the decimal, rather than dividing by 8 first.

**A decimal that is really a nice fraction, times a whole number.** $12.5 \times 480$: recognize $12.5 = \frac{1}{8} \cdot 100$. Divide 480 by 8 (getting 60), then multiply by 100:

$$
12.5 \times 480 = \frac{480}{8} \cdot 100 = 60 \cdot 100 = 6000
$$

**A percent as a fraction, applied to a number ("X% of Y").** $87\%$ of $5590$: $87\%$ isn't a clean eighths/thirds fraction, so on estimate questions like this you don't need the exact value — you need to land inside the given range. Round $87\%$ to something you can multiply fast, like $\frac{7}{8} = 87.5\%$:

$$
87\% \text{ of } 5590 \approx \frac{7}{8} \cdot 5590 = \frac{5590 \cdot 7}{8} \approx \frac{39130}{8} \approx 4891
$$

The exact value is $0.87 \times 5590 = 4863.3$, which lands inside the accepted estimate window — the fraction shortcut gets you there in a few fast mental steps instead of a full decimal multiplication.

**A percent over 100%.** $106.25\%$ of $640$: recognize $106.25\% = 1 + \frac{1}{16}$ (since $6.25\% = \frac{1}{16}$ of $100\%\text{)}$. Take the whole number plus one-sixteenth of it:

$$
106.25\% \text{ of } 640 = 640 + \frac{640}{16} = 640 + 40 = 680
$$

**Two decimals that are each nice fractions, multiplied together.** $0.0625 \times 0.32$: recognize $0.0625 = \frac{1}{16}$ and $0.32 = \frac{32}{100} = \frac{8}{25}$. Multiply the fractions:

$$
0.0625 \times 0.32 = \frac{1}{16} \cdot \frac{8}{25} = \frac{8}{400} = \frac{1}{50} = 0.02
$$

In each case the pattern is the same: spot the hidden fraction, rewrite the expression using it, then do the multiplication/division in whichever order (numerator first or denominator first) leaves you with the smallest, cleanest numbers to carry through by hand.

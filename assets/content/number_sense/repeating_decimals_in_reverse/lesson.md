# Repeating Decimals in Reverse

Another problem that has been in vogue in recent years is applying all the procedures used with repeating decimals (covered in the Repeating Decimals topic), but in reverse. Instead of giving you a decimal and asking for a fraction, they give you a fraction and ask for the first few digits of the decimal. Here is an example:

$$
\frac{23}{90} = 0.\underline{\hspace{2cm}} \quad \text{(first four digits)}
$$

There is really nothing new with these types of problems; you just have to work in reverse. Knowing the denominator is 90, the repeating fraction is in the form $.a\overline{bb}\ldots$ From here, you are trying to find an $a$ and $b$ such that $ab - a = 23$, where $ab$ is a two-digit number (not $a \times b\text{)}$. Here, it's quick to see that $ab = 25$ and you got your answer of **2555**.

The only tricky thing a test writer can do is give you a fraction where some reduction has taken place. Take for example:

$$
\frac{17}{45} = 0.\underline{\hspace{2cm}} \quad \text{(first four digits)}
$$

Here, the denominator has been reduced by a factor of 2, so you need to multiply by $\dfrac{2}{2}$ to get the fraction in a form you can work with:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{17}{45} \\
&= \frac{34}{90} \Rightarrow ab - a \\
&= 34 \Rightarrow ab \\
&= 37 \Rightarrow \textbf{Answer: 3777}
\end{aligned}
$$
